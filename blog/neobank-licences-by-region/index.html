the licence map

In America a neobank rents a bank. In Asia it is one.

November 11, 2026 · 5 min read · ← all posts · the dataset

"Neobank" sounds like one kind of company. Look at what the 382 we track are actually licensed to do, region by region, and it turns out to mean very different things depending on where the app was built.

The region is the market each neobank is filed under in the dataset. The 65 apps built for everywhere at once, mostly self-custodial wallets, are filed as global and left out of this table.

America: the app on top of someone else's bank

In the US, the typical neobank is not a bank. Thirty-six of the 53 we track, including Chime, Current, Dave, Mercury and Ramp, run on a sponsor bank that holds the deposits and the charter. The eight that do hold a licence are either the rare fintech that got its own charter (Varo, the first, in 2020; SoFi, by buying a bank in 2022), digital brands of existing banks (Ally, Marcus), or small chartered banks built for a niche.

The reason is structural. The US has no dedicated digital-bank licence. A fintech that wants to be a bank needs a full charter, with years of capital, examination and political risk, or it buys an existing bank. Renting a sponsor bank was the fast path, and for a decade almost everyone took it. We have written about what that costs customers when the sponsor changes, and about who actually holds the money when something breaks.

Asia: regulators built a door

Asia is the mirror image. Thirty-nine of its 68 neobanks hold a banking licence, because regulators across the region created licences specifically for them:

  • South Korea licensed internet-only banks: KakaoBank and K bank opened in 2017, Toss Bank in 2021.
  • Hong Kong granted eight virtual bank licences in 2019, which produced ZA Bank, Mox and WeLab Bank.
  • Singapore awarded digital bank licences in 2020: GXS, MariBank, ANEXT.
  • The Philippines approved six digital banks in 2021, including Tonik, GoTyme, UnionDigital, UNO and Maya.
  • Malaysia issued five digital bank licences in 2022, now live as GXBank, AEON Bank, Boost Bank and Ryt Bank.

Where that door doesn't exist, Asia looks like America. India has not issued digital-bank licences, and eight of Asia's eleven partner-bank neobanks, among them Jupiter, Niyo and Open, are Indian.

Britain and Europe: two ladders

The UK splits almost exactly in half: nine licensed banks (Monzo, Starling, Atom, Zopa, Kroo and others) and eight e-money institutions (Tide, Curve, ANNA). Britain's regulators set up a dedicated path for new banks in the mid-2010s, and the challengers that took it are now the licensed half. In continental Europe the e-money licence does more work: one licence passports across the single market, which is why 17 of the 61 European neobanks, Wise among them, are e-money firms rather than banks.

why it matters to youA licensed bank's deposits are insured directly. A partner-bank app's deposits are insured through the bank behind it, and that protection depends on the app's records being right. An e-money balance is safeguarded, not insured. The same-looking app can carry three different kinds of protection depending on the country. Every profile here says which one applies.

The US is changing

The American number is the one most likely to move. Since January, Nubank and Revolut have won conditional approval for national bank charters, and in September Chime agreed to buy its own sponsor bank. The partner-bank model that defined American neobanking for a decade is starting to give way. That's next week's post.
