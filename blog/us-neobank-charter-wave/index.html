the charter wave

America's neobanks are buying the bank. One bank bought a neobank.

November 18, 2026 · 5 min read · ← all posts · the dataset

Last week we showed that 36 of the 53 American neobanks we track run on someone else's bank, and only eight hold a licence of their own. Then September happened. In one month the biggest American neobank agreed to buy its sponsor, the biggest European one won a path to a US charter, and a bank went the other way and bought a neobank.

Chime: from tenant to landlord

Chime is the clearest case because it is the archetype of the partner-bank neobank: the largest in the US, built entirely on The Bancorp and Stride. Buying Stride, renamed Chime Bank, N.A. once the deal closes, turns the company into a bank holding company supervised by the Fed. That brings direct access to the deposits it already gathers and an end to paying a partner for the privilege.

Stride sponsors other neobanks too. Albert is one of them in our data. A sponsor bank owned by a competitor is a different counterparty from an independent one, and that is worth watching.

Nubank and Revolut: arriving as banks

The two largest neobanks outside the US are skipping the renting phase, or trying to. Nubank, with 139M customers at home, launched American products in September on Lead Bank while its own charter is organised. Revolut filed in March and got conditional approval six months later. The approval is a first step, not permission to take deposits: the FDIC, the Federal Reserve and a final OCC decision still stand between it and a US bank. Both are betting that in America the charter is the moat. Without one they would be two more apps on a sponsor bank. With one they compete with the incumbents on the same footing.

Bluevine: the other direction

Valley National's deal for Bluevine runs the opposite way, and it explains why the charter matters. Valley gets $2.1 billion of small-business deposits that cost less than its own, about 175,000 business customers and an engineering team. Bluevine's customers get a bank: their deposits move onto Valley's balance sheet within months of closing, and the partner-bank arrangement ends. For a neobank without a charter, being bought by a bank is one of the ways this story ends.

what changes for customersOn a partner-bank app, FDIC insurance reaches you through the sponsor's records of who owns what. That's the layer that failed in the 2024 Synapse collapse. When the app is the bank, the insured deposit sits on its own books. None of these deals has closed yet, so nothing has changed today. The direction is clear, though: the largest US neobanks are moving their customers' money from rented infrastructure to owned.

What we'll track

Our records move as these deals close, not when they are announced. Today Chime is still a partner-bank neobank in the dataset, with the Stride deal noted on its profile. Revolut's US approval and Bluevine's sale are noted on theirs. When a charter becomes final or a deal completes, the licence field changes and the changelog says so. When the Stride deal closes, the largest neobank in America moves from the partner-bank column of last week's table into the licensed one, as a company that started as an app on someone else's bank.
